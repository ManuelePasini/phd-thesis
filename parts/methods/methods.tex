\chapter{Methods}
\label{chap:methods}
{\color{red}
\Cref{chap:dataplat} introduced a reference architecture for domain-level data platforms, organized around a small set of composable modules for ingestion, storage, data processes, management, and exploitation. Within the data processes module, it also distinguished between platform processes, maintained by the platform itself, and user processes, the application-specific pipelines through which individual Digital Twins consume and produce data. That architecture, however, is deliberately abstract: it specifies which functionalities a data platform must provide, not which concrete technology should implement each of them. In practice, cloud providers such as Amazon, Google, and Microsoft expose ecosystems of dozens of overlapping, interoperable services, and selecting the right subset for a given application is, in current practice, left to the expertise of individual designers or consultants. The result is an unstructured, largely non-reproducible process, at odds with the standardization this thesis has argued for throughout.

This chapter addresses this gap, presenting a methodology that systematizes the selection of the concrete services instantiating the modules of a data platform, driven directly by the data-intensive processes the platform is meant to support. In terms of the platform/user process distinction introduced in \Cref{sec:dataplat-refarch}, the methodology takes as input a description of an application's user processes, which for a Digital Twin correspond to its own analytical or operational data pipelines, and derives, systematically rather than by expert intuition alone, the subset of services from a given ecosystem that implements them at minimal cost, while respecting dependency, compatibility, and mandatory constraints the designer may impose. Fittingly, the resulting characterization of a data platform is the same one adopted throughout this thesis (\Cref{chap:data_oriented_perspective}): a centralized infrastructure providing independent and composable services meeting the end-to-end needs of data pipelines.
}

\begin{partsummary}
{\color{red}
This chapter addressed two methodological aspects complementing the data architecture and the data model presented in \Cref{part:architectures}: how a data platform can be designed starting from the processes it must support, and how the metadata describing its data can be made accessible to its users. Both contributions should be understood as approaches to these problems, and as first steps in their direction, rather than as complete and final solutions.

The reference architecture of \Cref{chap:dataplat} specifies which functionalities a data platform must provide, but leaves the choice of the services implementing them to the expertise of individual designers. The process-driven design methodology of \Cref{sec:process-driven-design} makes this step systematic, deriving the optimal set of services from the data flows the platform must support, so that the resulting blueprint depends on explicit inputs and constraints and can be compared across providers. This extends standardization from the architecture of a data platform to its instantiation. Moreover, since the methodology accepts data flows from multiple stakeholders, the same procedure can derive the blueprint for one Digital Twin or for a set of them, consistently with the view of standardization at the individual level as scalable to an ecosystem.

The methodology, however, has several limitations. The most relevant concerns costs, arguably the most important aspect when designing a platform on a cloud ecosystem: the number of selected services is used as a proxy for cost, but pricing schemes, instance sizing, and expected workload, which largely determine the actual cost of a blueprint, are not modelled, so the blueprint returned as optimal is not necessarily the cheapest. Moreover, the service ecosystem must be mapped and kept up to date manually, organizational and political constraints can only be partially expressed, and the validation relies on a few real case studies assessed qualitatively by experts. Finally, the methodology does not yet cover the services required to manage the platform's metadata, which are the subject of the second contribution of this chapter.

Similar considerations apply to the investigation of LLM-assisted metadata query answering presented in \Cref{sec:llm-guided-metadata-exploration}. The investigation was carried out on a data warehouse, but the approach is not specific to it. It relies on three ingredients: a model graph describing the concepts of a modelling formalism, a schema graph instantiating it, and an LLM answering natural language questions over both. These ingredients apply equally to the representations of Digital Twin data proposed in this thesis, such as the domain-level conceptual schema of \Cref{sec:dataplat-datamodel} or the spatio-temporal property graph of \Cref{chap:stgraph}. Moreover, the benchmark provides a first, indirect test of the semantic level of interoperability discussed in \Cref{sec:challenges}, where independently developed systems refer to the same concepts under different names. Dirty queries reproduce precisely this situation: they refer to the concepts of the DW schema through synonyms rather than through the names under which those concepts are actually modelled, and the accuracy obtained on them, particularly for search queries, shows that GPT was often able to reconcile the two, identifying the intended concepts despite the different naming. This result, however, should be regarded as very preliminary. The mismatch was introduced on the side of the user's question, rather than between the schemas of two independently developed systems; the evaluation involved a single, synthetic schema and a limited number of queries; and the hallucinations observed on concepts with overlapping meaning, such as the categorization of brands and that of products, show that the reconciliation of similar but distinct concepts, which is precisely the most delicate case in semantic interoperability, remains unreliable. Establishing whether, and under which conditions, LLMs can support semantic alignment across independently developed Digital Twins requires a dedicated and substantially more extensive investigation.
}
\end{partsummary}
