Throughout the course of its evolution, human society has been continuously and profoundly shaped by the advancement of technology.
%
Since the turn of the millennium, the rise of digital technologies has transformed the way we live, work, and communicate, propelling humankind into a new digital era.
%
As society transitioned toward this digital paradigm, the concept of data began to gain value, ultimately becoming pivotal in what is now referred to as a digital society.
%
While this digital transformation continues to unfold, driven by the widespread adoption of cloud infrastructures, smart services, and the Internet of Things (IoT), the volume of generated data has exponentially grown \mpa{\{some\_ref\}}, presenting unprecedented opportunities alongside critical challenges in data management.
%
The availability of large-scale data, together with the remarkable growth in computational capabilities~\mpa{{some\_ref}}, has enabled the practical adoption of advanced data-intensive technologies that were once primarily confined to theoretical research.
%


Among these, digital twins have emerged as one of the most promising paradigms for bridging the physical and digital worlds.
%
A digital twin (DT) is a digital representation of a physical entity, process, or system that continuously mirrors its real-world counterpart, driving real-time interventions based on collected data.~\mpa{{some\_ref}}
%
By combining real-time observations with computational models and analytics, DTs enable advanced simulation, predictive modeling, and performance optimization of the physical entity within a virtual and risk-free environment.

As reflected by their progression along the Gartner Hype Cycle~\mpa{{some\_ref}}, DTs have evolved from an emerging research concept into an adopted technological paradigm across some application domains including manufacturing, healthcare, smart cities, and agriculture.
%
However, this growing maturity has also exposed significant limitations that currently hinder their large-scale adoption.~\mpa{{some\_ref}}
%
Most existing digital twins have been developed as domain-specific, vertically integrated solutions, independently designed to address the requirements of individual application scenarios.
%
As a result, they rely on heterogeneous software architectures, data representations, and application-specific information models, leading to fragmented data environments in which the information generated by individual digital twins remains largely confined within isolated silos.
%
This fragmentation hinders the integration and reuse of data across independently developed digital twins, ultimately limiting their interoperability, scalability, and applicability to more complex, cross-domain scenarios.
%
Achieving interoperability is therefore becoming a fundamental requirement for the next generation of digital twin systems.
%
Addressing this challenge involves two complementary yet distinct dimensions.
%
The first concerns the software infrastructure supporting digital twins. As current implementations are typically developed as self-contained solutions, there is a need for common architectural foundations capable of supporting the deployment, integration, and management of heterogeneous digital twins within shared environments.
%
The second concerns the data management and semantic dimension. Even when digital twins can be deployed within a common infrastructure, the heterogeneity of the data they produce and consume, together with the use of domain-specific data models, schemas, and representations, poses significant challenges to data integration.
%
Data originating from different digital twins may differ not only in their structure and format, but also in their temporal characteristics, granularity, metadata, and underlying semantics, making their integration and joint querying a non-trivial data management problem.
%
Addressing this challenge requires common data models and mechanisms for data integration and governance capable of providing consistent representations of heterogeneous information while preserving its provenance, context, and meaning across different digital twin applications.
%
These two dimensions provide complementary foundations for the development of interoperable digital twin ecosystems: the former addresses how digital twins can be integrated at the architectural level, while the latter addresses how the information they produce can be meaningfully integrated at the data level.
%
The importance of these foundations is likely to become even more pronounced with the recent emergence of large language models and increasingly capable agentic systems.
%
By combining access to heterogeneous information with advanced reasoning and decision-making capabilities, such systems may enable digital twins to evolve from primarily reactive representations of physical systems toward more proactive entities capable of autonomously analyzing information, initiating actions, and, ultimately, interacting with other digital twins.
%
Emerging visions of interconnected digital twins forming distributed "Digital Brains" further illustrate the potential of this evolution.
%
However, realizing such scenarios requires digital twins to first overcome their current fragmentation. The ability of intelligent agents to reason over information, communicate with other agents, and coordinate actions ultimately depends on the existence of interoperable underlying systems and shared representations of the data they exchange.

This thesis approaches these challenges from a data-oriented perspective, arguing that the development of interoperable digital twin ecosystems should be grounded in the data they collect, produce, and exchange.
%
Rather than considering the digital twin primarily as a standalone software application, the work first investigates the characteristics and requirements of the underlying data, using them as the basis for identifying suitable data models and mechanisms for their integration and management.
%
From this perspective, establishing common data models and, where possible, standardized representations for digital twins constitutes a fundamental step toward interoperability.
%
Once these data requirements and representations have been established, the corresponding software infrastructure can be designed according to the characteristics of the data and the operational requirements of digital twin systems.
%
This includes supporting heterogeneous data sources, different data models and formats, varying collection frequencies, and continuous data streams, while providing the scalability and architectural capabilities required by distributed digital twin deployments.
%
The thesis therefore follows a bottom-up perspective, moving from data characteristics and requirements, through data modeling and management, toward the architectural foundations required to support interoperable digital twin ecosystems.
%
This perspective points toward the need for dedicated software infrastructures capable of bringing these elements together, supporting the deployment and management of multiple digital twins while providing the data management capabilities required by their heterogeneous and continuously evolving data.
%
Such infrastructures can be envisioned as a Digital Twin Platform: shared software environments in which heterogeneous digital twins can be deployed and managed, and whose architecture is shaped by the characteristics and requirements of the data they collect and produce.
%
Rather than treating data management as a supporting component of the platform, this perspective places data at the core of its design, providing a foundation for addressing the architectural requirements of interoperable digital twin ecosystems.

\paragraph{Goals of the thesis}

The overarching goal of this thesis is to contribute to the development of interoperable Digital Twin ecosystems from a data-oriented perspective. The research focuses on understanding the data management challenges arising from Digital Twin applications and on identifying the models, technologies, and architectural foundations required to address them.
%
Along this line, the goals of this thesis can be concisely summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item characterize the data generated and managed by Digital Twin applications, identifying their main characteristics, requirements, and the challenges they pose to existing data management technologies;

    \item investigate data models, data management approaches, and governance mechanisms capable of supporting the integration and interoperability of heterogeneous Digital Twin data;

    \item investigate the architectural foundations required to deploy and manage heterogeneous Digital Twins, contributing toward the realization of Digital Twin Platforms capable of satisfying their data and operational requirements.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these goals follow the bottom-up perspective adopted throughout the thesis, moving from the characteristics and requirements of Digital Twin data toward the data models, management mechanisms, and architectural foundations required to support interoperable Digital Twin ecosystems.
